The Best Neighborhoods in State College, PA

By Barb Alpert, REALTOR®, Associate Broker — Kissinger Bigatel & Brower

There is no single best neighborhood in State College. There is only the one that fits how you actually spend your days — your commute, your budget, whether you want to walk to a coffee shop or hear nothing but wind. After thirty years of showing homes here, here is my honest read on each.

First, what do people mean by “State College”?

The borough itself is small. Most of what people call State College is actually College, Ferguson, Harris and Patton Townships wrapped around it. That distinction matters more than newcomers expect: it changes your municipal taxes, your services, and sometimes your school attendance area, and none of it shows up on a map app. Ask which municipality any home is in before you fall in love with it.

Which neighborhoods are best if you want to walk everywhere?

College Heights is the classic answer. Just north of Park Avenue, it is mature, tree-lined, architecturally interesting and genuinely walkable to campus. It is also the most consistently in-demand neighborhood in the county, which shows in the price and in how rarely homes come available. If you want it, be pre-approved and ready to move the week it lists.

Holmes-Foster, south and west of downtown, is my other walkable favorite. Restored early-twentieth-century homes mixed with solid mid-century houses, a strong neighborhood identity, and an easy walk to both downtown and campus. Turnover is low, so listings there come up less often than you would expect for the size of the neighborhood.

The Highlands, between downtown and campus, is the most urban-feeling part of the county. It is a genuine mix of owner-occupied homes and student rentals, and it varies block by block. That is not a warning so much as an instruction: walk the specific block on a Thursday evening before you decide.

Which neighborhoods offer more space and newer construction?

Park Forest, in the northwest, is mid-century single-family housing on generous lots, and it remains one of the more attainable ways into the State College Area School District. The houses are solid and many have been updated well. If your budget will not stretch to College Heights, it is worth comparing.

Gray’s Woods, in Patton Township toward Port Matilda, is newer construction — larger contemporary homes, its own elementary school, and quick access to I-99. It suits buyers who want modern layouts and low maintenance and do not need to walk downtown.

Toftrees, north of town off Waddle Road, is built around the golf course and offers a lot of townhomes and condominiums alongside single-family homes. It is popular with buyers who want to lock the door and travel, and with people who value the I-99 access.

What if you want more land or a shorter price tag?

Look outward. Pine Grove Mills in Ferguson Township gets you village quiet against Tussey Ridge, still in SCASD, about ten minutes from campus. Boalsburg gives you a genuinely historic village and quick access to the medical center and airport. Bellefonte, fifteen or twenty minutes northeast, is where your budget goes furthest — substantial Victorian homes at prices that do not exist on the campus side.

And if what you actually want is acreage, Centre Hall and Penns Valley over the mountain is a different market entirely, with the county’s best farm and horse property.

How much should schools drive your decision?

For many buyers here, a great deal — the State College Area School District is a major driver of demand across the whole area. Two practical cautions. First, attendance boundaries are set by the district, they do change, and listing data lags behind; verify any specific address with the district directly. Second, districts other than SCASD serve much of the county and have their own strengths, so do not rule out Bellefonte Area or Penns Valley Area without looking.

I will happily talk through school district facts, boundaries and what the buyer pool responds to. What I will not do is rank neighborhoods by the people who live in them — that is a fair housing line, and it exists for good reason.

What actually decides it?

In my experience, three things: the drive you will make every single day, whether the house works for the life you have now rather than the one you had five years ago, and how the street feels at seven in the evening. Everything else is negotiable.

Drive the commute at the real commute hour. Visit on a home football Saturday if you can, so you know what that is like. And walk the block, not just the house.
