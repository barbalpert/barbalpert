Buying a Horse Farm in Centre County, PA: A Local Expert’s Guide

By Barb Alpert, REALTOR®, Associate Broker — Kissinger Bigatel & Brower

I have been riding for more than forty years and keeping horses at home for a good share of that. So when I walk a farm with a buyer, I am not only looking at the house. I am looking at where the water runs in March, whether a truck and trailer can turn around, and what that fence line is going to cost you in three years.

Here is what I have learned matters most when buying a horse farm in Centre County, in roughly the order it will affect your life.

How much land do you actually need?

The figure people quote is about two acres of good pasture per horse, and in our part of Pennsylvania that is a reasonable planning number for grazing without destroying the ground. But the important word is usable. A listing that says twenty acres may mean eight acres of flat, fenceable pasture and twelve acres of steep woods. Beautiful, and not turnout.

Walk the ground yourself, ideally in mud season. Look at the grade — a gentle slope sheds water and is a gift, while low flat ground that holds water becomes a mud pit by February. Ask what has been done for pasture management, because a field that has been overgrazed for a decade takes real time and money to bring back.

You can absolutely keep horses on less than two acres each. Plenty of people around here do it well on three to five acres total, using a sacrifice paddock and feeding hay. It just means more active management and more money spent on hay and footing.

What should you look for in the barn?

Structure first, cosmetics never. Paint and stall mats are cheap. A failing bank barn foundation, rotted sills or a roof at the end of its life are not, and they are easy to miss when you are charmed by the beams.

Then the practical dimensions: twelve-by-twelve stalls and a ten- to twelve-foot aisle are comfortable to work in. Many older Centre County bank barns run tighter than that, because they were built for smaller animals and different farming. That does not disqualify them, but you should know before you buy whether your horses will actually fit and whether you can get a wheelbarrow past a cross-tied horse.

Check for water and electric at the barn. A frost-free hydrant is worth a great deal on a January morning, and running a new water line to a barn is a genuine expense. Look at the wiring honestly, look at where hay is stored relative to it, and think about whether a fire truck could reach the building.

Is an indoor arena worth paying for?

In Pennsylvania, yes — if you ride through the winter. An indoor arena is the single biggest value driver on a horse property here and by far the most expensive thing to add later. Between site prep, drainage, base, the building itself, and potentially zoning and stormwater approval, building one is a major project. Buying a farm that already has one lets you finance it inside the mortgage.

When you evaluate any arena, indoor or outdoor, ask about the base rather than the surface. Footing can be amended for a few thousand dollars. A bad base has to be excavated and rebuilt. Find out what is underneath and when it was done. For outdoor rings, ask how long it takes to dry after rain — a ring you cannot use for three days after a storm is a ring you will not use.

What about fencing, water and septic?

Walk the entire perimeter. Post-and-rail, no-climb mesh, coated wire and vinyl all behave differently and fail differently. Rotting posts and sagging line are a per-foot replacement cost you can price out before closing and negotiate on. Barbed wire is a real hazard for horses, and if it is there, plan to replace it.

On water: horses drink a lot, often eight to twelve gallons a day each and more in heat or hard work. Test the well’s flow rate, not just the water quality. Our limestone valleys give hard water; test for nitrates and bacteria regardless. Have the septic inspected and find out where the drain field sits before you plan a new paddock over top of it.

What zoning and tax questions should you ask before you offer?

This is where people get hurt. Every township in Centre County writes its own rules on animal density, boarding, lessons and accessory structures. If you intend to board horses or teach, get written confirmation from the township zoning officer before you close. Never assume the previous owner’s use transfers to you.

Also confirm whether the parcel is enrolled in Clean and Green, Pennsylvania’s preferential assessment program for agricultural and open-space land. It saves real money on taxes, but subdividing or changing the use can trigger roll-back taxes for prior years plus interest. Ask about conservation easements, agricultural security area status, and any right-of-way over the farm lane — including who plows it.

Where should you be looking?

The best equestrian ground in the county is in the valleys. Centre Hall and Penns Valley offer the most acreage per dollar and genuine farm ground. Boalsburg and Harris Township have smaller farmettes at a premium for the short commute. Bellefonte, Spring and Benner Townships offer strong value, and Pine Grove Mills has some of the prettiest small holdings anywhere here.
